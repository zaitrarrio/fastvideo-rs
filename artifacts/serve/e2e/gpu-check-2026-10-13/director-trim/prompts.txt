THE CEDAR HALL — a series bible.  Logline. High above a pine valley, in a mountain retreat built of honey-coloured cedar, a small community of animals keeps a daily practice of stillness, breath and balance. The retreat is old, its floorboards worn smooth by generations of quiet feet, and its tall windows look out over ridgelines that turn blue, then violet, then gold as the day moves. The series follows one season in the hall: the arrival of storms, the departure of a beloved teacher, the small rivalries and kindnesses of students, and the slow way a restless young student learns to be still. The tone is warm, gently comic, and sincere; nothing cruel happens here, but feelings run deep and the weather is never entirely tame.  World. The Cedar Hall stands on a granite shelf at the end of a switchback trail. The main room is long and high, with exposed beams, iron lanterns hung on chains, sheer cream curtains that breathe with the draughts, and three great arched windows facing the valley. Practice mats are laid in rows on the polished floor, dark slate-blue against the amber wood. A brass bowl of incense always burns at the front of the room, its smoke rising in thin ribbons that curl when anyone moves. Potted ferns and a small lemon tree crowd the corners. Behind the hall there is a covered deck of weathered planks that hangs out over the drop, a rain barrel, a woodshed, and a narrow path to a spring where the students fetch water at dawn. In the far distance a single blue mountain, the Old Shoulder, dominates every view; its snowcap is the retreat's clock and calendar.  Daily rhythm. The bell rings before sunrise. Students fetch water, sweep, and light the lanterns. Morning practice is long and silent. Midday is chores and a simple meal of seeds, berries and broth. Afternoon practice is shorter and includes balance work: holding difficult postures on one leg, on the deck rail, on stepping stones in the spring. Evening is storytelling by lantern light. The rhythm is strict but never harsh; the teacher believes that a schedule is a kind of shelter.  Sound world. Wind in pines, the creak of cedar, the soft crackle of incense, rain on the roof shingles, distant thunder rolling around the Old Shoulder, the low resonant tone of the brass bowl when struck, frogsong at dusk from the spring, and long, comfortable silences. Music, when it appears, is a single bamboo flute played somewhere off screen.  Visual language. Warm, natural light from the windows is the key light in nearly every scene; lanterns add amber pools at night. The camera is patient: slow dollies along the rows of mats, gentle push-ins on faces, occasional high wide shots from the rafters that make the students look like a small garden. Weather changes the palette: storms bring slate greys and cold blue rim light; clear evenings bring peach and rose. Smoke from the incense is a recurring motif, used to show stillness (straight ribbons) and disturbance (swirling eddies).  Characters.  Master Bramble is the old teacher of the hall, ancient by the reckoning of the students, broad and heavy and very calm, with deep amber eyes that seem half closed and fully awake at the same time. He speaks rarely and briefly, often in questions. He has taught in the hall for longer than anyone remembers and he is beginning, privately, to think about who will keep the bell after him. His great gift is patience; his weakness is that he hides his own tiredness from everyone.  Pip is the youngest student, small, bright green, quick and fidgety, with enormous curious eyes. Pip arrived at the start of the season after a long climb from the valley and wants desperately to be good at stillness, which is the one thing Pip is worst at. Pip counts breaths out loud, peeks at the others during silent practice, and falls off the deck rail during balance work at least once a day. Pip is also brave, honest, and the first to help anyone in trouble.  Juniper is a senior student, poised and proud, an expert at the hardest balances, who privately believes she should be the next keeper of the bell. She is sharp with Pip at first, then slowly protective.  Old Moss is the caretaker, who rarely practises but keeps the lanterns trimmed and the woodshed full; he knows every board in the hall that creaks and every leak in the roof.  The Rows are the dozen or so ordinary students who fill the mats, each with small habits the camera notices: one who always sits too close to the incense, one who falls asleep, two who giggle.  Season arc.  Part one, Arrival. Pip comes up the trail at dusk in rain, soaked and nervous, and is shown a mat at the end of the last row. The first morning practice is a disaster of small noises. Master Bramble says nothing, but places the incense bowl closer to Pip's mat.  Part two, The Storm Season. Late summer storms roll in from behind the Old Shoulder. Thunder rattles the arched windows, lanterns swing on their chains, and the curtains billow inward. Practice continues through the storms; Master Bramble teaches that the storm is outside and the stillness is inside. Pip is terrified of thunder and struggles. Juniper, who is not afraid of anything, mocks and then quietly sits beside Pip during the worst of it.  Part three, The Deck. Balance work moves outside onto the rain-washed deck above the drop. The planks are slick, the wind is strong, and the valley below is full of cloud. Pip falls again and again, always caught by someone. Old Moss mends the rail. Master Bramble watches from the doorway, leaning on the frame more than he used to.  Part four, The Teacher's Tiredness. One morning the bell does not ring on time. The students find Master Bramble sitting very still at the front of the hall, awake but too weary to rise. Juniper rings the bell. The hall understands, without anyone saying it, that a change is coming.  Part five, Clear Sky. After the last storm the sky opens; sunlight floods the hall and turns the incense smoke gold. Pip, finally, holds a one-legged balance on the deck rail for a full breath, then another, while the whole hall watches through the arched windows. Master Bramble smiles for the first time in the season.  Rules of the world. No one in the hall ever shouts. Practice is never cancelled for weather. The incense is lit before the bell and never allowed to go out during practice. The deck is used only for balance work. Students bow to the Old Shoulder when they first see it each morning. The keeper of the bell is chosen by the teacher, but only after the hall has already, silently, chosen.  Recurring images. The brass bowl and its smoke. The arched windows with the Old Shoulder framed in the centre. Rows of slate-blue mats. Lanterns on chains. Wet footprints on cedar. A single fern leaf trembling when thunder hits. Pip's enormous eyes peeking open during silent practice.  Episode notes for directors. Keep the camera calm even when the weather is not. Let silences run. Begin scenes wide and settle in. Prefer reactions to action: the most important events in this series are small shifts of attention. Comedy comes from Pip's restlessness against the hall's stillness, never from humiliation. When in doubt, return to the incense smoke.  Extended location notes. The front of the hall: a low dais of darker wood, the brass bowl on a small stand, two iron lanterns on chains, a scroll with a single brushed character meaning 'breath'. The windows: three tall arched frames with thin mullions; the central one has a crack in its upper pane that Old Moss has taped. The side walls: shelves of folded blankets, bundled incense, a water jug. The doorway to the deck: a heavy sliding cedar door that rumbles on its track. The deck: planks grey with weather, a rail of pole wood, a view straight down into cloud. The spring: a stone basin among ferns, cold and loud, with stepping stones used for balance practice on calm days. The trail: switchbacks of wet granite and roots, marked by small cairns.  Extended character notes. Master Bramble's movements are slow, deliberate and economical; when he lifts a hand the room goes quiet. He wears nothing and needs nothing; his authority is entirely in his stillness. Pip blinks often, tilts the head when listening, and drums toes on the mat when nervous. Juniper sits taller than anyone, chin lifted, and holds balances with an effortless calm that she has worked very hard to make look effortless. Old Moss hums under his breath and smells of woodsmoke. Among the Rows, the dozing student is called Fennel, the incense-hugger is called Ash, and the giggling pair are the twins Reed and Rush.  Themes. Stillness is not the absence of movement but the presence of attention. Teachers grow tired; communities carry them. The young are not wrong to be restless; they are early. Weather passes. The hall remains.  Continuity notes. The season runs from late summer into early autumn; the light gets lower and warmer as it goes. The Old Shoulder's snowcap grows through the season. The crack in the central window pane is always there. The lemon tree in the corner fruits in part four. Pip's mat moves one row forward in each part of the season, until in part five it is in the front row beside Juniper.  Tone references. Quiet nature documentaries, slow cinema, gentle animated features about small communities, the pacing of a long exhale. Nothing frantic.
